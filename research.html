Our research

From the physics of quantum phase slips to practical microwave devices — we build and measure superconducting circuits for quantum technologies.

Quantum phase slip nanowires

In ultra-thin, strongly disordered superconducting nanowires, the superconducting phase can slip by 2π through quantum tunnelling. Coherent quantum phase slips (CQPS) are the exact charge–flux dual of Josephson tunnelling, opening a route to new qubits, charge-based interferometers and a potential quantum current standard.

Key questions

  • How can we engineer the phase-slip energy reproducibly through film thickness, width and disorder?
  • What limits coherence in QPS circuits, and how do we mitigate it?
  • Can QPS elements complement Josephson junctions as building blocks for quantum circuits?

Approach

Sputtered NbN thin films patterned by electron-beam lithography into nanowires tens of nanometres wide, embedded in microwave circuits and characterised at millikelvin temperatures.

Superconducting resonators & gate-tunable CQUIDs

High-kinetic-inductance coplanar waveguide (CPW) resonators are both a probe of our nanowires and devices in their own right. By shunting a λ/4 resonator with two QPS nanowires and coupling a gate electrode, we form a charge quantum interference device (CQUID) whose resonance frequency can be tuned with voltage — the dual of a flux-tuned SQUID.

Key questions

  • What are the dominant loss mechanisms (two-level systems, quasiparticles, radiation) in our resonators?
  • How large a voltage-tuning range can we achieve while preserving high quality factors?
  • How do kinetic inductance nonlinearities shape the resonator response?

Approach

Resonator design with electromagnetic simulation, notch-type coupling to a feedline, and transmission (S21) measurements using vector network analysers and lock-in techniques as a function of gate voltage, power and temperature.

Parametric amplification & nonlinear dynamics

Reading out superconducting qubits and weak microwave signals requires amplifiers that add as little noise as quantum mechanics allows. We explore nonlinear superconducting circuits — including Josephson- and phase-slip-based devices — as parametric amplifiers, using both four-wave (Kerr/Duffing) and voltage-pumped three-wave mixing.

Key questions

  • Can a gate-pumped CQUID provide three-wave-mixing gain with useful bandwidth and dynamic range?
  • How do bifurcation and Duffing dynamics limit gain and saturation power?
  • How do measured gain profiles compare with circuit-level models?

Approach

Pump–signal experiments with microwave sources and spectrum analysis, combined with numerical models that map physical circuit parameters onto normalised nonlinear-oscillator simulations.

Quantum sensing applications

The extreme sensitivity of superconducting circuits to charge, flux and microwave photons makes them natural quantum sensors. We investigate how tunable resonators and parametric devices can be applied to sensing and low-noise detection.

Key questions

  • Which sensing tasks benefit most from charge-tunable superconducting devices?
  • How can on-chip amplification improve detector sensitivity?

Approach

Device-level benchmarking of noise and responsivity alongside collaboration with application partners.

How we work

Facilities & methods

We use the shared cleanroom and measurement infrastructure of the London Centre for Nanotechnology.

Nanofabrication

Thin-film deposition, electron-beam lithography and etching to define nanowires and microwave circuits.

Cryogenics

Dilution refrigerators reaching ~10 mK with filtered, attenuated microwave and DC wiring.

Microwave measurement

Vector network analysis, lock-in detection, RF sources and spectrum analysis for device characterisation.

Modelling & analysis

Electromagnetic simulation, circuit modelling and Python-based data analysis pipelines.
